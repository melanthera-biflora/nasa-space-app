# Turn Climatch into a seasonal trend detective

Climatch should make the **monthly series its analytical core**, with the yearly Mann-Kendall number kept as the headline. Under it go a covariance-corrected Seasonal Kendall test with a seasonal Sen's slope for "is anything changing?", per-season and per-month trends corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg for "which part of the year is changing?", an autocorrelation-safe test on anomalies, and a **"years needed" estimate** that turns every non-significant result into the challenge's own lesson that a longer series may be needed. All of it runs in well under a second with pymannkendall and scipy, which are already in the project's environment, so the core upgrade needs no new statistics dependency. Weekly data should drive *looking* (smoke weeks, heat waves, frost dates), not p-values: weekly means carry no more information about the long-term trend than monthly means and add autocorrelation. For regions, NASA POWER's regional endpoint returns a state-sized MERRA-2 grid without a login in about half a second. That makes **US-state averages**, the region type in the challenge video, live for temperature, precipitation, soil wetness and frost days. MODIS AOD, the video's own variable, needs a one-time precompute or a Giovanni CSV export. Disasters belong on a separate, switchable event layer fed by free catalogs (NCEI Storm Events, OpenFEMA, US Drought Monitor, NOAA HMS smoke; NASA EONET only from about 2013). Each trend should come with a "with and without event years" check so a 2023 smoke spike cannot pass as a trend. The redesign should be a Next.js 16.4 App Router app whose pages follow the challenge steps (choose, look, seasons, test, events). Analysis state lives in the URL, the hand-written SVG charts stay, and one `rewrites()` rule sends `/api/*` to the unchanged FastAPI backend.

A note on evidence: results labelled "own simulation" come from small Monte Carlo runs (Gaussian AR(1) noise, 200–400 replicates, so each rejection rate has a standard error of about ±0.015) made with the project `.venv` during this research. The scripts `fp_mk.py`, `bench_mk.py` and `years_needed.py` sit in the temporary session scratchpad at a local scratch folder (not in the repo). Copy them into the repo if the team wants to keep the numbers.

## Monthly series need tests that respect seasons and weather memory

Today the app computes `monthly_anomaly()` (each month minus that calendar month's mean) but runs OLS and Mann-Kendall only on yearly values, with `min_years=8` ([local trends.py](../data_pipeline/analysis/trends.py)). That headline is sound. In our own simulation, Mann-Kendall on 25 annual means built from autocorrelated monthly noise rejected a true null 4.3% of the time at month-to-month persistence φ = 0.2 and 7.5% at φ = 0.5, close to the nominal 5%, because averaging to years removes most of the persistence (own simulation, `fp_mk.py`). The weakness is not the p-value but what it hides: an annual mean averages away change that happens in only one season, which is exactly the pattern the deeper app is meant to surface.

### One overall monthly test, then the seasons that drive it

The right overall test for monthly data is the **Seasonal Kendall test**, which compares only January with January and February with February, so the seasonal cycle cannot fake a trend ([Hirsch, Slack & Smith, USGS OFR 81-488](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1981/0488/report.pdf); [Helsel et al. 2020, USGS TM 4-A3 ch. 12](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/tm4A3)). pymannkendall provides `seasonal_test`, `correlated_seasonal_test` and `seasonal_sens_slope`, and for seasonal tests a full cycle counts as one time step, so the slope comes out **per year** ([pyMannKendall README](https://github.com/mmhs013/pyMannKendall)). The covariance-corrected version matters. In our simulation the plain `seasonal_test` falsely flagged a trend **11% of the time at φ = 0.2 and 21% at φ = 0.5**, while `correlated_seasonal_test` stayed at 6% and 5%. Adjacent months share weather, and the plain test treats the 12 monthly statistics as independent (own simulation, `fp_mk.py`). It costs about 29 ms on 300 months (own benchmark, `bench_mk.py`).

The installed source adds three traps the API must guard against. The seasonal functions reshape the series to years × 12 and pad only at the end, so **the series must start in January**. `correlated_seasonal_test` drops every year that has a single missing month. `original_test` silently drops NaNs, which compresses the time axis and changes the units of Sen's slope (local read of `.venv/Lib/site-packages/pymannkendall/pymannkendall.py`). The Seasonal Kendall test also assumes all seasons move the same way: if summers warm and winters cool, the two cancel and the test reports nothing. The overall verdict therefore must always sit beside a season-level view.

That view runs Mann-Kendall plus Sen's slope separately on each calendar month's ~25 yearly values (about 1 ms each) and corrects across them with Benjamini-Hochberg, using the `scipy.stats.false_discovery_control` call the US trend map already uses ([local trend_field.py](../data_pipeline/tools/trend_field.py)). The correction is not optional. With 12 independent tests at α = 0.05, the chance that at least one month looks significant by luck is **46%**; with 4 seasons it is 19%, and with 52 weeks 93%. Wilks's review of atmospheric-science practice recommends false-discovery-rate control as the principled, cheap fix ([Wilks 2016, BAMS, AMS abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/96Annual/webprogram/Paper276937.html)). The default view should be **four seasons (DJF, MAM, JJA, SON)**, with the 12-month bars as a zoom-in: four tests carry a lighter multiple-testing burden, and each seasonal value averages three months of noise. December belongs to the following winter, so the first, incomplete DJF of 2001 must be dropped. To claim "summers are warming faster than winters", test the per-year series JJA − DJF directly. One significant season beside one non-significant season does not show they differ.

### Anomaly tests must correct for weather memory

When the input is the deseasonalized monthly anomaly series, autocorrelation inflates false alarms badly. Serinaldi & Kilsby document "a general increase of the rejection rate" of no-change tests under serial correlation ([Serinaldi & Kilsby 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-015-1041-5)). Our simulation on 300 monthly anomalies with no true trend gave these false-alarm rates at α = 0.05 (own simulation, `fp_mk.py`):

| Test (pymannkendall) | φ = 0.2 | φ = 0.5 | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `original_test` | 0.105 | 0.230 | Never on monthly data |
| `hamed_rao_modification_test(lag=3)` | 0.060 | 0.060 | **Default for anomalies** |
| `pre_whitening_modification_test` | 0.045 | 0.060 | Fallback when Hamed-Rao returns NaN |
| `yue_wang_modification_test` (default lag) | 0.490 | 0.520 | Never with default `lag=None` |
| `trend_free_pre_whitening_modification_test` | 0.115 | 0.320 | Avoid |
| `correlated_seasonal_test` (raw monthly) | 0.060 | 0.050 | **Default overall test** |

Hamed-Rao with its default all-lags setting returned p = NaN in one benchmark run because its corrected variance went negative, so the code needs a guard (own benchmark, `bench_mk.py`). The existing OLS 95% confidence interval assumes independent residuals. On monthly data it is too narrow by a factor of √((1+φ)/(1−φ)), which is **×1.22 at φ = 0.2 and ×1.73 at φ = 0.5** ([Holbert, power derivation under AR(1)](https://www.cfholbert.com/blog/mann-kendall-power-deriavation/)). Either widen it by that factor or drop OLS intervals on monthly data. For skewed, positive variables (AOD, PM2.5, NO2, fire counts), report Sen's slope divided by the series median as **% per year**, so places compare fairly.

### "Not significant" becomes "about N years needed"

The challenge says a non-significant trend may only need a longer series. Weatherhead's work makes that sentence quantitative. The years needed to detect a trend grow with the noise variance and its autocorrelation. Weatherhead's own slides give n ≈ [(2σN/|ω0|)·√((1+φ)/(1−φ))]^(2/3) for a 50% chance of detection at 95% confidence ([Weatherhead & Hinkelman 2006, NASA CERES slides](https://ceres.larc.nasa.gov/documents/STM/2006-05/weatherhead_CER0605.pdf); [Weatherhead et al. 1998 abstract](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/factors-affecting-the-detection-of-trends-statistical-considerati/)). Replacing 2 with 3.3 gives a 90% chance, the form Henson et al. used to show that satellite chlorophyll needs about 40 years to separate a climate trend from natural variability ([Henson et al. 2010](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/7/621/2010/)). The 3.3 attribution came from a search snippet. It agrees with the generic z-value derivation (1.96 + 1.28) ([Holbert](https://www.cfholbert.com/blog/mann-kendall-power-deriavation/)). For a trend of 0.3 units per decade, the 90% version gives:

| Monthly anomaly SD σN | φ = 0 | φ = 0.2 | φ = 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 14.5 yr | 16.6 yr | 20.9 yr |
| 1.0 | 23.0 yr | 26.3 yr | 33.1 yr |
| 1.5 | 30.1 yr | 34.4 yr | 43.4 yr |
| 2.0 | 36.4 yr | 41.7 yr | 52.6 yr |

These are own computations (`years_needed.py`). A realistic single-place warming of 0.3 °C per decade therefore needs **roughly 25–45 years** to reach p < 0.05 nine times out of ten, which is longer than the app's 25-year window. Computing σN and φ from the user's own detrended anomalies costs microseconds. The card can then say: "With this much natural up-and-down, a change this size usually needs about 30–40 years to stand out. We have 25." Round the answer, because σN and φ are themselves estimates. Real records also have ENSO and drought-year variability that the simulation lacked, so true needs run longer than the table.

The literal fix is also available. POWER meteorology runs from **1981 to the present** ([POWER llms.txt](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/llms.txt)), and the MERRA-2 monthly collections in CMR start in 1980 ([CMR M2TMNXAER](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?collection_concept_id=C1276812866-GES_DISC&sort_key=-start_date&page_size=1)). A "Try a longer series" button that extends POWER variables back to 1981 lets students watch a borderline trend become clear, which is the challenge's point made tangible. Add one caveat line: longer reanalysis records cross changes in the observing system, which can create jumps unrelated to the Earth.

The wording rules follow from this. Say "not clear yet", never "no trend" (the app already uses "Not significant yet"). Never call a p-value "the chance the trend is real"; say "how often chance alone would give a change this big". Always state the period, never extend the line past the data, and label MERRA-2 values as model-plus-observation estimates.

## Season shape, weekly views and extremes are where students find patterns

The most engaging results do not come from a better p-value. They come from turning each year into a few "season shape" numbers and trend-testing those 25 values with plain Mann-Kendall and Sen's slope. Each takes under 1 ms. These numbers answer questions students actually ask ("is spring coming earlier?").

**Frost dates and season length** come straight from POWER daily T2M_MIN. The last spring frost is the last day before July 1 with a minimum at or below 0 °C, the first fall frost is the first such day after July 1, and the frost-free season is the gap between them. This is the EPA definition. EPA reports that the contiguous-US season has grown more than two weeks since the early 1900s and that the last spring frost now comes earlier than at any time since 1895 ([EPA growing-season indicator](https://epa.gov/climate-indicators/climate-change-indicators-length-growing-season)). POWER also serves a ready-made monthly `FROST_DAYS` parameter (Madison: 146 frost days in 2025) ([POWER FROST_DAYS test](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/monthly/point?parameters=FROST_DAYS&community=ag&longitude=-89.4&latitude=43.07&start=2024&end=2025&format=JSON)). Three caveats apply. Return "not applicable" where frost never or always occurs. MERRA-2 grid-cell minima are smoother than station minima, so dates will differ from local records. And frost-date series are noisy because each date is a single day.

**Seasonal amplitude and timing** reward a little math. JJA mean minus DJF mean per year is the explainable version ("the summer-winter gap is shrinking"). The first-harmonic amplitude and phase from 12 monthly values give timing with sub-monthly precision. Stine, Huybers & Fung found that the annual temperature cycle over extratropical land shifted **1.7 days earlier between 1954 and 2007**, and that such changes matter even when the annual mean does not move ([Stine et al. 2009, RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v457y2009i7228d10.1038_nature07675.html); [Berkeley release](https://newsarchive.berkeley.edu/news/media/releases/2009/01/21_seasons.shtml)). Show the phase only when the first harmonic explains most of the seasonal variance. This is our rule of thumb; a bimodal cycle makes the phase meaningless.

**Fire season** from FIRMS uses the dates by which 10%, 50% and 90% of a year's detections occurred, with length = d90 − d10. Require about 20 detections per year. The Southeast's two burning seasons need percentiles, not harmonics, and detections are a proxy shaped by overpasses and clouds, not burned area. Globally, fire-weather season length rose 18.7% from 1979 to 2013 ([Jolly et al. 2015, RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v6y2015i1d10.1038_ncomms8537.html)), which gives students a published benchmark to compare against.

### Weekly series show events but add no trend power

Weekly data feel more detailed, but for a trend in the mean they add nothing. In our simulation, 25 years of autocorrelated daily noise gave the **same fitted-slope spread, 0.101 per decade, whether aggregated to days, weeks, 28-day months or years**. Weekly means carried lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.26, while monthly means had almost none. The analytic Weatherhead standard error predicted 0.103 per decade, which matches (own simulation, `years_needed.py`). A weekly Seasonal Kendall test with period 52 also takes about 450 ms in its corrected form (own benchmark). The split is therefore clean: **weekly series are for seeing and monthly or annual series are for testing.** Show weekly anomaly lines, weekly extreme-day counts and smoke weeks, but never 52 per-week p-values.

Build weeks as fixed 7-day blocks from January 1, with day 365/366 folded into week 52. ISO weeks give some years 53 weeks and straddle year boundaries, which breaks week-of-year statistics. Rolling 7-day means are display-only because overlapping windows manufacture autocorrelation. Weekly precipitation is zero-inflated, so count wet or heavy-rain days instead of fitting raw totals. One limit is the request budget. POWER daily regional calls cap at 366 days ([live 422 test](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/daily/regional?latitude-min=42.5&latitude-max=47.1&longitude-min=-92.9&longitude-max=-86.8&parameters=PRECTOTCORR&community=ag&start=20020101&end=20241231&format=JSON)), so 24 years of daily data for one state and one variable is 24 calls of about 0.5 s each. That must be precomputed or cached after the first request.

### Anomalies, extreme days and records turn values into "how unusual"

Calendar-month z-scores (value minus that month's baseline mean, divided by its SD) power an **anomaly heatmap**, a recoloured version of the existing month-by-year heatmap where a band of red recent Julys jumps out. For skewed variables, use percentile ranks within the calendar month or z-scores of logs. State the baseline, because a trend inside the baseline shrinks recent z-scores. Extreme-day counts follow the ETCCDI TX90p idea: count days above the local 90th percentile for that calendar day, computed over a 5-day moving window ([Copernicus CDS extreme indices](https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/sis-extreme-indices-cmip6)). Under no change about 10% of days, roughly 3 per month, exceed it, which gives students a built-in null.

Records give the most intuitive null. Without warming, record highs and record lows would be about equally common. From January 2000 to September 2009 the contiguous US set **291,237 daily record highs against 142,420 record lows, about 2:1** ([AGU press release on Meehl et al.](https://news.agu.org/press-release/daily-record-high-temperatures-occurred-twice-as-often-as-record-lows-over-past-decade/)). A per-year count of record-high and record-low days in the student's own series reproduces that test. Under standard probability, year t of a steady series sets a record with chance 1/t. Count only from about year 11 and label the results dataset records, not official station records.

### Change points and STL are tempting but premature

Skip change-point detection for the hackathon. Serial correlation distorts the Pettitt test even more than it distorts Mann-Kendall ([Serinaldi & Kilsby 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00477-015-1041-5)), and any steady trend yields a "most likely break" near the middle of the series. PELT needs a penalty choice that the ruptures docs give no guidance on ([ruptures PELT docs](https://centre-borelli.github.io/ruptures-docs/user-guide/detection/pelt/)), and ruptures is not installed. STL decomposition (`statsmodels.tsa.seasonal.STL`) is a good *picture* of trend, season and remainder ([statsmodels STL](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.tsa.seasonal.STL.html)). But statsmodels is not in `requirements.txt`, STL cannot handle gaps, and significance tests on its smoothed trend component are invalid by construction. It is a stretch goal, precomputed, behind an "Advanced" disclosure.

## State averages run live through POWER, but AOD needs a precompute

The challenge video averages over **a state**, not a point, so Climatch should add the state as a region type beside its existing address points. NASA POWER makes this cheap. Its regional endpoint returns the native MERRA-2 0.5° × 0.625° grid for a bounding box **at least 2° and at most 10° on each axis, one parameter per call**. A Wisconsin monthly T2M request for 2002–2024 returned 100 grid cells in 0.54 s with no login ([POWER monthly regional test](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/monthly/regional?latitude-min=42.5&latitude-max=47.1&longitude-min=-92.9&longitude-max=-86.8&parameters=T2M&community=ag&start=2002&end=2024&format=JSON); [10° limit error](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/monthly/regional?latitude-min=24&latitude-max=50&longitude-min=-125&longitude-max=-66&parameters=T2M&community=ag&start=2020&end=2020&format=JSON); [2° minimum error](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/monthly/regional?latitude-min=43&latitude-max=44&longitude-min=-90&longitude-max=-89&parameters=T2M&community=ag&start=2020&end=2020&format=JSON)). Texas, California, Montana and Alaska need 2–4 tiled calls. Rhode Island, Delaware, Connecticut and DC need a padded 2° box masked back to the polygon. The mask comes from the Census 2024 cartographic boundary file `cb_2024_us_state_20m.zip` (188 KB, public domain) ([Census download](https://www2.census.gov/geo/tiger/GENZ2024/shp/cb_2024_us_state_20m.zip); [data.gov record](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/2024-cartographic-boundary-file-shp-state-and-equivalent-entities-for-united-states-1-2000)). Take a cos(latitude)-weighted mean of the cells whose centres fall inside. Counties are too small for a 0.5° grid, so use county FIPS only to match events, not as a data region. POWER publishes no numeric rate limit but can return 429 and warns against repeated identical requests ([POWER API overview](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/services/api/)), so cache every state pull.

AOD is the hard part, and it is the variable the challenge names. MYD08_M3 v6.1 (MODIS Aqua, monthly 1°) runs from July 2002 to October 2026, but each global granule is about 375 MB of HDF4, so **288 months is roughly 108 GB**, not a laptop job ([CMR MYD08_M3](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?collection_concept_id=C1443775657-LAADS&sort_key=-start_date&page_size=1)). Giovanni is still running in November 2026 at release 4.34, and its web UI offers an "Area-Averaged Time Series" ([Giovanni 4.34 release](https://disc.gsfc.nasa.gov/information/service-release?title=Giovanni+Release+4.34+Features+a+New+Look+to+Choosing+a+Data+Visualization); [Giovanni User Guide](https://giovanni.gsfc.nasa.gov/giovanni/doc/UsersManualworkingdocument.docx.html)). But GES DISC staff say its time-series API works only for single points ([Earthdata Forum, via search snippet](https://forum.earthdata.nasa.gov/viewtopic.php?t=7628)). The fastest faithful replica is therefore **a manual Giovanni CSV export of MYD08_M3 for about ten demo states**, stored as state-month rows. Two points are unverified: whether v4.34 still offers US-state shape masks, and how long each export takes. The scripted fallback is MERRA-2 `TOTEXTTAU` from M2TMNXAER. It is NetCDF4, about 80 MB per month and streamable with earthaccess, but it is model-plus-assimilation rather than pure MODIS ([CMR M2TMNXAER](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?collection_concept_id=C1276812866-GES_DISC&sort_key=-start_date&page_size=1)). For weekly smoke spikes, the daily 0.05° MAIAC product MCD19A2CMG at 44 MB per day ([CMR MCD19A2CMG](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?collection_concept_id=C2565807733-LPCLOUD&sort_key=-start_date&page_size=1)) can be pulled for one summer only (about 4 GB for June–August 2023) rather than the whole record.

Finally, POWER advises ending climate-trend analyses about two months before real time, because the newest period comes from the GEOS-IT extension ([POWER data sources](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/methodology/data/sources/)).

## Disasters go on a labelled layer, not into the trend

Event detection from one place's POWER daily data takes a few lines of pandas per method. Heat and cold flags use calendar-day 90th-percentile thresholds in the ETCCDI TX90p style ([Copernicus CDS](https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/sis-extreme-indices-cmip6)) plus run rules: **3 or more days for a heatwave** (Perkins & Alexander) or 6 or more for the warm-spell index WSDI ([ANU repository](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/on-the-measurement-of-heat-waves/); [ETCCDI 27 indices](https://etccdi.pacificclimate.org/list_27_indices.shtml)). The Excess Heat Factor needs only daily mean temperature and about six lines ([Nairn & Fawcett 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4306859)). Dry and wet spells are CDD and CWD run lengths of `PRECTOTCORR`, drought is SPI from the `climate_indices` package ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/climate-indices)), and monthly outliers are the calendar-month z-scores that already feed the anomaly heatmap. Two framing rules apply. Using 2001–2025 as both baseline and analysis period is in-sample, so every flag should read "relative to this place's 2001–2025 climate". And the NWS has no single national heatwave definition, with thresholds that vary by office ([Midwestern Regional Climate Center](https://mrcc.purdue.edu/living_wx/heatwaves); [NWS Albany heat fact sheet](https://www.weather.gov/media/aly/FactSheets/NYSHeat.pdf)), so the app should never imply it matches an NWS warning.

| NIFC perimeters | Through the 2024 season | ArcGIS Open Data ([NWCG](https://nwcg.gov/publications/pms936/national-incident-feature-services/public-distribution)) | Fire footprints |

NOAA retired the billion-dollar product in May 2025 and froze it at 2024 ([PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/u-s-stops-tracking-costs-of-extreme-weather-disasters-fueled-by-climate-change)). Climate Central relaunched it on 22 October 2025 under the same lead scientist and method ([Fox Weather](https://www.foxweather.com/weather-news/noaa-billion-dollar-disaster-database-returns-climate-central)). EONET's earliest US results in a 2001–2014 bounding-box query were 2013 storms ([EONET query](https://eonet.gsfc.nasa.gov/api/v3/events?status=all&start=2000-01-01&end=2014-12-31&bbox=-125,50,-66,24&limit=50)), so it cannot label 2001–2012 spikes despite being the NASA-branded option. Smoke flags need care. Burke et al. count a PM2.5 anomaly as smoke only when HMS imagery shows a plume overhead ([Burke, Childs et al. 2023](https://web.stanford.edu/~mburke/papers/BurkeChilds_etal_2023.pdf)), and counting light HMS plumes greatly overstates smoke days and their trends ([Wildland Fire WF23148](https://www.publish.csiro.au/WF/fulltext/WF23148)).

The "visible" column follows from grid arithmetic. A POWER/MERRA-2 cell of 0.5° × 0.625° ([POWER data sources](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/methodology/data/sources/)) covers about 2,700–3,000 km² at mid-latitudes. The Camp Fire's ~620 km² is a fifth of one cell, and the two LA fires together are about 5%. Synoptic events (heat domes, cold outbreaks, droughts, long-range smoke) appear at full strength, while fire footprints and convective rain maxima are smoothed. Time averaging also hides events: one 3-day event is at most 10% of a monthly mean. Daily detections should therefore feed monthly views as **event-day counts per month**, not only through monthly means. Treat the gridded magnitudes as relative, not as exposure. MERRA-2 PM2.5 has underestimated surface peaks against monitors ([ACP 2021](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/21/14427/2021/)), and transport models overestimated western smoke peaks by up to 3–5× ([PubMed 39694472](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39694472/)). The expected ranks, such as June 2023 being the top June for Wisconsin PM2.5, still need to be measured in Climatch's own data before the demo script promises them.

### Show that one spike is not a trend, and that spikes can become one

Sen's slope with Mann-Kendall already resists a single outlier year: the Theil-Sen estimator tolerates corruption of up to about **29.3%** of points ([scikit-learn Theil-Sen example](https://scikit-learn.org/1.5/auto_examples/linear_model/plot_theilsen.html)). Four cheap checks make that resistance visible. A **leave-one-out** pass recomputes the slope 25 times, each time without one year, and calls the trend "fragile" if any single removal flips its sign or crosses p = 0.05. A with-and-without view shows the slope with event years and without them, side by side. An endpoint warning fires when the record maximum or minimum falls in the first or last three years, where it has the most leverage. A frequency test trends the count of exceedance days separately from the mean, so "are spikes getting more frequent?" gets its own answer apart from "is the typical level changing?". That last check matters because events and trends are not always opposites: wildfire smoke now drives PM2.5 trends across much of the West and is reversing decades of air-quality gains ([Stanford News 2022](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2022/09/wildfire-smoke-unraveling-decades-air-quality-gains)). Label a spike "matched" only when a catalog event of a compatible type overlaps it in time (±3 days for daily data, the same month for monthly data) and in space (the county or state). Otherwise say "unusual, no matching catalog event". Every event card says "consistent with", never "caused by", and links to official AQI or NWS pages instead of giving health advice.

## Charts and pages should walk the challenge's five steps

Perception research sets the chart rules. Position on a common scale is judged most accurately, and colour is judged least accurately ([Cleveland & McGill summary, Cornell INFO3312](https://info3312.infosci.cornell.edu/slides/04-graphical-perception.html)). Charts where students judge a trend should therefore be aligned line charts. The existing heatmap and climate stripes ([Hawkins et al. 2024](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/122245/8/bams-BAMS-D-24-0212.1.pdf)) serve as overviews only. Radial charts read worse than linear ones even for cyclic data ([Waldner et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1907.13534)), so climate spirals are out. Proma et al. found trellis small multiples did worse than plain lines for trend tasks ([arXiv 2510.11912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11912)). Their trellis split one series into chunks, unlike a seasonal cycle plot, but the result argues for always pairing the cycle plot with the plain line.

| Student question | Primary chart | Overview or add-on |
|---|---|---|
| Is it changing overall? | Monthly or annual line, Sen or OLS trend with a **shaded** 95% band | Stripes header |
| Which seasons or months are changing? | Cycle plot: 12 (or 4) mini-series side by side, shared y-axis ([Cleveland cycle plot, DescTools](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/DescTools/html/PlotMonth.html)) | Trend-per-season bars, faded when q ≥ 0.05 |
| Did the season shift? | Early-decade vs late-decade mean annual cycle on one Jan–Dec axis | Frost-date or fire-season timeline |
| Is this month or year unusual? | Band chart: normal range as 10–90th and 25–75th percentile bands, this year in bold ([Climate Reanalyzer](https://climatereanalyzer.org/clim/t2_daily/); [NOAA CPC](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/bulletin_1206/figs4.html)) | Rank among all same months |
| When do extremes happen? | Anomaly heatmap (z or percentile), cells with \|z\| ≥ 2 outlined | Event icons in matched cells |

Uncertainty needs the same discipline. Draw the trend's confidence region as a shaded band, not whisker error bars, which invite all-or-nothing reading ([Correll & Gleicher 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6214189)). Keep the band visible whenever the line is shown, because showing a mean without its uncertainty leads people to discount uncertainty ([Kale, Kay & Hullman](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/effect-size-judgments)). The verdict should have two plain states: **"Clear trend"** when p < 0.05, worded as "a change this large would rarely happen by chance", and **"Not clear yet"** otherwise, followed by the direction, the "about N years needed" sentence and a "Try a longer series" button. That matches the existing label and avoids a middle tier that reads like "almost significant". Wherever 12 months or many map cells are tested, add one sentence: "We tested 12 months, so we used a stricter bar." Free exploration multiplies spurious insights with every comparison ([Zgraggen et al., CHI 2018](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137892)). The detective flow should therefore ask students to guess before they test, and could show how many comparisons they have made. Hypothetical outcome plots helped untrained viewers spot weak trends ([Kale et al., HOPs](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/hops-trends)) and make a good stretch "which line is the real data?" activity.

Reference tools agree on structure. NOAA Climate at a Glance and Our World in Data both use one control set (place, variable, period) over several views (time series, map, rankings, table), keep the view state in the URL, and put downloads beside each chart ([NCEI CAG](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/monitoring/climate-at-a-glance/); [OWID redesign](https://ourworldindata.org/redesigning-our-interactive-data-visualizations)). EPA indicator pages follow a fixed order: Key Points, then About the Indicator, then figures ([EPA Heat Waves](https://www.epa.gov/node/263455)). That order answers the challenge's call for simple definitions. Segel & Heer's "martini glass" structure fits the worked-example requirement: a short author-driven story that hands off to free exploration with the controls pre-filled ([Segel & Heer 2010](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)). Bret Victor's reactive documents add the idea that the numbers in sentences update with the chart ([Explorable Explanations](https://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/)).

| Route | Detective step | Content |
|---|---|---|
| `/` | Case files | Challenge framing, 3–4 demo cases (Wisconsin AOD 2023 first), "Start your own" |
| `/explore` | Choose and look | Region and variable pickers with one-line definitions, period presets, time series with stripes header, "up, down or neither?" guess |
| `/seasons` | Find the pattern | Cycle plot, season and month trend bars with FDR, anomaly heatmap, frost dates or fire-season timeline |
| `/test` | Test the trend | Overall Seasonal Kendall verdict, anomaly test, years needed, longer-series button, fragility checks |
| `/events` | Spikes vs trend | Band chart, rankings, event layer and event cards, trend with and without event years |
| `/map`, `/compare` | Zoom out | Existing FDR grid map (click a cell to open `/explore`), 2–3 places on aligned axes |
| `/about` | Methods and data | Definitions, sources, caveats; the household questionnaire becomes an optional "Personalize" step |

A sticky context bar carries region, variable and period across pages and turns into a bottom sheet on phones. Every chart needs a short set of accessibility features. Put a one-sentence takeaway generated from the data above the chart; it doubles as the long description that W3C asks for ([W3C WAI complex images](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex)). Add `role="img"` with `aria-describedby`, a "Show data table" toggle with a single header row, keyboard-focusable points and no colour-only significance. On phones, annotations move below the chart as numbered notes ([Datawrapper](https://blog.datawrapper.de/better-more-responsive-annotations-in-datawrapper-data-visualizations/)).

## Next.js 16.4 can wrap the FastAPI backend with one rewrite

The target is **Next.js 16.4.6 with React 19.3.1** on Node 20.9+ ([npm registry: next](https://registry.npmjs.org/next); [Next.js 16.4 blog](https://nextjs.org/blog/next-16-4)). Next 16 made Turbopack the default, removed synchronous `params` and `searchParams` and removed `next lint` ([Next.js 16 blog](https://nextjs.org/blog/next-16)). The blog shows frequent security releases, so pin the exact patch version. The official Vite migration guide gets the existing app running unchanged as a client-only SPA inside an optional catch-all route, `app/[[...slug]]`, using `dynamic(..., { ssr: false })`. Real routes are split out afterwards ([Next.js: Migrating from Vite](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/guides/migrating/from-vite)). Climatch must deviate in one place. The guide sets `output: 'export'`, which disables rewrites ([Next.js: Static Exports](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/guides/static-exports)), so drop that line and add the proxy instead:

```js
// next.config.mjs
const API_ORIGIN = process.env.API_ORIGIN ?? 'http://127.0.0.1:8000'
export default {
  async rewrites() {
    return [{ source: '/api/:path*', destination: `${API_ORIGIN}/api/:path*` }]
  },
}
```

Rewrites act as a URL proxy and also apply to client-side routing ([Next.js: rewrites](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/api-reference/config/next-config-js/rewrites)). The existing `api/` layer can therefore keep calling relative `/api/v1/...` URLs with no CORS changes and no `NEXT_PUBLIC_` variables. Server Components that fetch directly must use the absolute `API_ORIGIN`, because no browser origin exists on the server. Route Handlers would only add a hop. Leave **Cache Components off** for the hackathon, even though 16.4 enables it in new apps. It requires a `<Suspense>` boundary around every uncached read and explicit handling of `Date.now()` and `Math.random()` ([Next.js: Caching](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/getting-started/caching)). Those rules cost migration time, while FastAPI and Supabase already cache the data.

Put the page type in the path and the analysis state (`region`, `var`, `from`, `to`, `season`) in search parameters, read with **nuqs 2.10.3**. nuqs has a Next App Router adapter and a `createLoader` for the server side ([nuqs adapters](https://nuqs.dev/docs/adapters); [nuqs server-side](https://nuqs.dev/docs/server-side)). Every client component that reads search parameters on a prerendered route must sit inside `<Suspense>`, or **the production build fails**. Dev mode hides this ([Next.js: useSearchParams](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/api-reference/functions/use-search-params)), so run `next build` daily. Layouts receive no `searchParams`. URL values are user input, so allowlist `var` and clamp month and week numbers before they reach FastAPI.

Keep Leaflet 1.9.4 as plain imperative code inside one `useEffect` that calls `map.remove()` on cleanup. Load it through `dynamic(..., { ssr: false })` in a `'use client'` file, because `ssr: false` is rejected in Server Components ([Next.js: Lazy loading](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/guides/lazy-loading)). react-leaflet 5.0.0 still has the open "Map container is already initialized" issue ([#1133](https://github.com/PaulLeCam/react-leaflet/issues/1133)), and Leaflet 2.0 is beta and ESM-only ([Leaflet 2.0 alpha](https://leafletjs.com/2025/05/18/leaflet-2.0.0-alpha.html)). Skip both. Keep the hand-written SVG charts too. Pure SVG built from presenter output can render as Server Components with zero chart JavaScript, leaving small client islands for hover and keyboard focus. The alternatives cost roughly 150 kB gzip for Recharts, 127 kB for Observable Plot and 368 kB for ECharts ([Bundlephobia API](https://bundlephobia.com/api/size?package=recharts@3.10.1)).

Tooling has its own small traps. **Vitest 5.1 lists `vite` as a required peer**, so Vite stays a devDependency even though the migration guide says to uninstall it ([npm registry: vitest](https://registry.npmjs.org/vitest)); the presenter tests carry over unchanged. Vitest does not support async Server Components, so those pages need Playwright end-to-end tests ([Next.js: Vitest guide](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/guides/testing/vitest)). openapi-typescript 7.13 requires `typescript@^5` ([npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/openapi-typescript)), which is enough to generate types from the backend's existing `openapi.v1.json` and import them through JSDoc. Hydration mismatches come mostly from dates and random IDs, so format dates in UTC with a fixed locale and use `useId()` for SVG gradients and clip paths ([Next.js: Hydration error](https://nextjs.org/docs/messages/react-hydration-error)). Every page needs a unique title, because the route announcer reads `document.title` first ([Next.js: Accessibility](https://nextjs.org/docs/architecture/accessibility)). `<ViewTransition>` page crossfades are optional; they work with no configuration but need an explicit reduced-motion CSS rule ([Next.js: View transitions](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/guides/view-transitions)).

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. The statistics the app needs already exist in its dependencies. The risk is not a missing method but **false discovery**: 12 months, several variables, weekly views and an event layer give students many chances to find something by luck. The design that holds up spends its comparisons on purpose. Seasons come before months, the FDR correction is explained in one sentence, students guess before they test, every trend shows its with-and-without-events sensitivity, and weekly data never gets p-values. The other shift is in the challenge's own weakest moment, the non-significant result. A per-place "years needed" figure, plus a button that actually extends POWER data back to 1981, turns that dead end into the lesson the challenge wants students to learn.

The architecture change is less risky than it looks. The presenters, API layer and SVG charts carry over, and FastAPI stays untouched behind a single rewrite, so the real work is the **contract**. The new analysis fields have to flow through `data_pipeline/analysis` and the report JSON before any page can show them. That ordering is how the project's own rules already require contract-first changes. Two things remain unverified and should be checked on day one: whether Giovanni still exports state-masked AOD CSVs, and whether the famous events actually rank first in Climatch's own gridded series. The demo story depends on both.
