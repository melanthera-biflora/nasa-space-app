# Re-cut global files to map North America

Climatch can extend its animated and trend maps to a **10–72°N, 170–50°W North America box** mostly by re-cutting NASA and NOAA global products it already downloads. Four small new inputs complete the set: NOAA OISST v2.1 monthly sea temperature (no login), NASA FIRMS MODIS fire files for Canada and Mexico, NOAA's ENSO and teleconnection index text files, and Natural Earth country outlines. On the MERRA-2 0.5° × 0.625° grid the app already paints, that box is **24,125 cells, 4.8 times the lower-48 box**. A full 25-year field with values and change measured **0.77–0.87 MB gzipped**, under the 2 MB page budget, so the JSON frames can stay. The core set (PM2.5, AOD with dust and organic carbon, HMS smoke, FIRMS fires, OISST, ENSO) needs about **0.5 GB of download and under 200 MB of new disk**. OMI NO₂ is the only multi-gigabyte item, about 0.9 GB if only the new strips are fetched. Border seams are not the risk: every recommended layer is one product across all three countries. The risks are breaks in time inside 2001–2025, and one code trap. The US mask is built from whatever FIRMS files are on disk, so adding Canadian and Mexican fire files silently grows the "US" into its neighbours unless the mask switches to the Census outline first. For climate drivers, NOAA's official ENSO index has been the **Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) since 1 February 2026**. The 25 years hold only about 7 El Niño and 12 La Niña winters, so the app should show composites with counts, not correlations.

## One product per field removes border seams, but not time breaks

The box and grid come straight from the existing code. The maps currently crop to the lower 48 (`BOX = {"lat": (24.0, 50.0), "lon": (-125.0, -66.0)}`) ([trend_field.py](../data_pipeline/tools/trend_field.py)). The MERRA-2 grid is 361 rows by 576 columns, which the AOD builder already addresses by row and column ([merra2_aod.py](../data_pipeline/sources/merra2_aod.py)). Cut at **rows 200–324 and columns 16–208**, the North America box is 125 × 193 cells. It takes in Mexico, Central America to northern Costa Rica, the Gulf of Mexico, Cuba and Hispaniola, Hawaii, all of mainland Alaska, Canada south of the Arctic islands, and Newfoundland. The southern edge matters for air quality, not just looks. Spring fires in the Yucatán and southern Mexico gave southern Texas its highest fine-particle levels since 1998 ([ScienceDaily 2006](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/10/061012185917.htm)). Smoke from central Mexico and the Yucatán can push several Texas metro areas over the O₃ and PM2.5 standards ([ACP 2023](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/23/10845/2023/acp-23-10845-2023.html)). Long-range transport needs its own wider views: about 0–40°N, 100–15°W for Saharan dust, and about 20–60°N, 100°E–110°W for Asian outflow, which crosses the dateline. These are better treated as separate "where it comes from" maps than as a bigger default box.

**One grid for land and sea is affordable.** A coarser ocean grid would save only about 0.6 MB gzipped per field. In exchange it would need two overlays, two hover look-ups and a visible seam at the coast. The existing `paint()`, `cellAt()` and `squareAt()` already take any lats, lons and cell size from the JSON ([fieldPaint.js](../frontend/src/lib/fieldPaint.js)). Fires stay as point counts per 1° square, as they are today ([trend_field.py](../data_pipeline/tools/trend_field.py)).

The design rule is **one product per field, everywhere on the map**. Global or continental products have no edge at the border. Those include MERRA-2, OMI, MODIS, FIRMS, GRACE, OISST and HMS, whose analysis covers "North America, Hawaii, and the Caribbean" ([NOAA OSPO HMS](https://www.ospo.noaa.gov/products/land/hms.html)). National products stop at the border and use different methods. SNODAS covers only the contiguous US ([NSIDC G02158](https://nsidc.org/data/g02158)). Canada's NBAC fire perimeters depend on agency methods that vary over time ([Hall et al. 2020](https://publish.csiro.au/wf/WF19201)). Mexico's CONAFOR fire polygons were found only for single years (2014, 2022) ([IDEFOR](https://idefor.cnf.gob.mx/layers/geonode%3Aincendios_2014_v1/pdf_metadata_layer)). National products like these belong in overlays or context text, never in a continuous colour field. Mixing a US product with a different Canadian one in the same field would draw a false cliff at the 49th parallel.

Global products remove seams in space, but they carry breaks in time, and a 2001–2025 trend map runs straight across them. The breaks the trend charts and captions must flag:

One more inconsistency has nothing to do with time. HMS analysts draw smoke by eye from GOES true-colour imagery, about twice a day, and the product "has not been fully validated" against surface data ([Liu et al., Harvard ACMG](https://acmg.seas.harvard.edu/sites/projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/acmg/files/liut_hms_isd.pdf)). Cloud and analyst workload differ by region, so smoke over remote northern Canada is probably under-recorded compared with the US. No source measures that bias, so the caption should say coverage is uneven rather than claim a size for it.

## Canada and Mexico need two fire files and wider crops, not new products

The stories from the neighbours are well documented. Jaffe and colleagues estimated about **16,000 extra US emergency-room visits** on 2023 smoke days, and Baltimore's average smoke PM2.5 reached 2.0 µg/m³ that year, against 0.7 over the previous four years ([ScienceDaily, Dec 2024](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/12/241210115914.htm)). The same early-season fires produced the highest regional surface ozone ever recorded across the northern US ([NOAA CSL](https://csl.noaa.gov/news/2024/418_1126.html)). On the Mexican side, smoke, dust and border-city emissions all cross into Texas, New Mexico and California. Every one of these can be drawn with products Climatch already uses or one widened crop away.

**Fire is the one place where new files are needed, and it carries the trap.** FIRMS serves yearly country CSVs with no login, and `_load_all()` merges every country parquet it finds. But `us_mask` builds the US outline from "every cell with at least one FIRMS detection", reading that same merged set ([trend_field.py](../data_pipeline/tools/trend_field.py), [fires_firms.py](../data_pipeline/sources/fires_firms.py)). Run `python -m sources.fires_firms Canada Mexico` before replacing the mask with the Census outline, and the US trend map quietly expands into both neighbours. The Canada series will also be dominated by 2023. Its 2023 MODIS file is 26.5 MB, against 2.6–5.9 MB in 2005, 2015 and 2019. Central American and Caribbean countries are separate files of about 0.7–1 MB a year each (Guatemala, Honduras, Cuba). Guatemala and Honduras are worth adding because they are major sources of the spring smoke that reaches Texas. VIIRS 375 m detections are sharper but start in 2012, and the 2023 Canada file alone is 140.6 MB. Keep VIIRS for a "recent detail" view, not for 2001–2025 trends ([FIRMS archive](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/download/)).

**Smoke works best as a pair of layers.** HMS polygons mark smoke anywhere in the column, so an HMS smoke day does not mean high surface PM2.5 ([NOAA OSPO HMS](https://www.ospo.noaa.gov/products/land/hms.html)). MERRA-2 surface organic and black carbon (OCSMASS, BCSMASS) gives the surface side, from the same M2TMNXAER files the PM2.5 build already reads. Only the summed PM2.5 is kept on disk today, so dust and organic carbon mean adding `DUSMASS25` and `OCSMASS` to the AOD builder's existing DAP4 request ([pm25_merra2.py](../data_pipeline/sources/pm25_merra2.py), [merra2_aod.py](../data_pipeline/sources/merra2_aod.py)). The same dust variables cover Chihuahuan Desert dust into New Mexico and west Texas, and Saharan dust reaching the Gulf Coast. MERRA-2 "adequately captures day-to-day Saharan dust variability" in Miami because it assimilates AOD ([AMS 2018](https://ams.confex.com/ams/98Annual/webprogram/Paper335459.html)). It does put too much dust in the larger size bins ([Kramer et al. 2020](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/20/10047/2020/)). Canada's NBAC 1972–2024 polygons ([CWFIS](https://cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca/datamart/download/nbac)) are the official Canadian burned area under the Open Government Licence. Use them as an optional outline overlay, not a colour field. The CAMS EAC4 reanalysis would be a useful second opinion on smoke, but it starts in 2003 and needs an ECMWF account ([ADS EAC4](https://ads.atmosphere.copernicus.eu/datasets/cams-global-reanalysis-eac4)). MERRA-2 is NASA, needs no extra registration and covers the whole window.

**Gases and heat take the cheapest route.** Climatch already builds OMI NO₂ at 0.25°. Extending it to Tijuana–San Diego, Ciudad Juárez–El Paso and Monterrey means changing `US_ROWS` and `US_COLS`, with no new product. TROPOMI Level 2 is thousands of swath files a year ([Earthdata](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog/ges-disc-s5p-l2-no2-hir-2)). For recent detail, use the HAQAST 0.1° monthly L3 grid instead ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/haqast-sentinel-5p-tropomi-nitrogen-dioxide-no2-global-monthly-level-3-0-1-x-0-1-degree-gr-b42b4)). TEMPO reaches from Mexico City to the Canadian oil sands hourly ([Earthdata TEMPO](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/learn/articles/tempo-overview)), but only from August 2023, so it suits a showcase, not a trend. For heat, **skip MODIS land surface temperature for the wider box**. It has no data over water, would cost about 3.6 GB of OPeNDAP transfer, and Terra's orbit drift ends usable trends in 2022 ([modis_lst_monthly.py](../data_pipeline/sources/modis_lst_monthly.py), [DATA_ADDITIONS.md](../docs/DATA_ADDITIONS.md)). MERRA-2 T2M covers land air temperature on the display grid for about 30 MB, and the same collection's TS gives a land-and-sea surface temperature.

**Water and snow come with resolution limits.** GRACE's real resolution is a 3° mascon, about 330 km, so a Great Lakes cell mixes lake level, groundwater and soil moisture on both sides of the border. Show it as a regional signal, not a lake. For snow cover, MOD10CM is the uniform NASA option. ERA5-Land is the best three-country snow water equivalent, but it needs an ECMWF account. SNODAS stops at the 49th parallel and the Rio Grande, so it would draw an artificial edge on a North America map. The gridded drought monitor is the only seamless three-country drought layer. The only grid route found runs through Earth Engine, so it remains a stretch goal unless the team rasterises NOAA's shapefiles itself. For Asian pollution reaching the West Coast, MOPITT total-column CO (1° monthly from March 2000) shows the spring trans-Pacific plumes ([ACP 2022](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/22/7193/2022/)). It needs the wider dateline-crossing box and stops in early 2025.

## The sea layer costs a few hundred megabytes, mostly without a login

Ocean data fill about half of the box, which is mostly sea. They are also the cheapest data on this list. Every core ocean product comes from NOAA servers with no login, and NASA's products use the Earthdata login the team already has. Only Copernicus Marine needs a separate (free) account.

| Seamless land-and-sea temperature | MERRA-2 TS | 0.5° × 0.625° · 1980 on ([CMR](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/variables.umm_json?name=TS)) | GES DISC | About 17.6 MB |

| Great Lakes ice | NOAA GLERL ice cover | per lake and grid · 1973 on | HTTPS, no login ([GLERL](https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/data/ice/)) | Annual-maximum file 2,243 bytes |

**OISST monthly is the right sea temperature product.** At 0.25° it puts about 2 × 2.5 source cells into each MERRA-2 cell, so averaging is clean. It covers all of 2001–2025 with one method. A worked-out OPeNDAP index request (`sst[232:1:531][400:1:599][880:1:1239]`) returns January 2001 to December 2025 for 10–60°N, 140–50°W. It was not test-downloaded, but the dimensions were checked live. The alternatives each fail one test. ERSST at 2° is too coarse for coastlines, and NCEI says it "smooths local and short-term variations" ([NCEI ERSST](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/extended-reconstructed-sst)). NASA's MUR starts on 31 May 2002 and so misses 2001 ([PO.DAAC MUR](https://podaac.jpl.nasa.gov/dataset/MUR-JPL-L4-GLOB-v4.1)). Coral Reef Watch CoralTemp joins three source products, with splices in 2002 and 2016 ([CRW](https://coralreefwatch.noaa.gov/product/5km/index_5km_sst.php)). MERRA-2's own TS is the cheapest seamless land-and-sea temperature, but it crosses the April 2006 forcing switch. Use it for yearly anomaly maps and OISST for trend lines. The CoastWatch ERDDAP server returned HTTP 503 on some test requests before working, so the demo should **cache every file locally and never depend on a live server**.

**The other ocean layers support specific stories.** For heatwaves, NOAA PSL's ready-made monthly files use OISST, a 90th-percentile threshold and a 1991–2020 baseline ([PSL MHW](https://psl.noaa.gov/marine-heatwaves/)). The pixel-level Hobday definition, at least five days above the 90th percentile, would need about 3.8 GB of daily OISST to compute (an estimate). The 2013–15 northeast Pacific "Blob" has a sourced chain to Western drought: a persistent Gulf of Alaska ridge both warmed the sea and pushed storms away from the West ([US CLIVAR](https://usclivar.org/node/873)). For sea level, the household story should use the nearest CO-OPS tide gauge. It measures **relative** sea level, including land sinking, which is what a house experiences ([CO-OPS](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/trends-and-extremes/)). Set the NASA-SSH offshore cell beside it, and the difference between them is mostly vertical land motion. For hurricanes, compute ACE from HURDAT2's 6-hourly winds, using only the 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC rows at tropical-storm strength or stronger. Including the extra landfall and peak rows double-counts ([NOAA CPC](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/outlooks/Background.html)). Great Lakes ice cover is a strong, tiny winter series, with lows in 2002 and 2012 and records such as Lake Erie's 98.2% in 1994 ([GLERL](https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/data/ice/)). For atmospheric rivers, IVT = √(UFLXQV² + VFLXQV²) sits on the same grid as the land layers. Ready-made MERRA-2 AR masks end in mid-2017 ([UCAR ARTMIP](https://data.ucar.edu/dataset/5ab98e63-14df-4717-a1a0-e415f2fb1916)). Chlorophyll and the Lake Erie bloom index make good side stories, but they show weak "ocean drives US climate" links. The bloom index was also reprocessed in May 2025, so older published values disagree with current ones ([NCCOS](https://coastalscience.noaa.gov/?p=118362)).

## Climate drivers are kilobyte text files that support only modest claims

Every large-scale pattern index is a free NOAA text file covering 2001–2025 completely. Download each file once, normalise it to an `index, year, month, value` table, and clip the display at December 2025. The files already hold 2026 values, including an El Niño reading of 1.97 for August–October 2026.

| Index | File | Coverage and notes | Role in the app |
|---|---|---|---|

| Relative Niño-3.4, monthly | [CPC Rnino34.ascii.txt](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/data/indices/Rnino34.ascii.txt) | Monthly input to RONI | Monthly correlations |
| ONI (legacy, still updated) | [CPC oni.ascii.txt](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/data/indices/oni.ascii.txt) | Seasons from 1950 | Robustness check |
| MEI.v2 | [PSL meiv2.data](https://psl.noaa.gov/enso/mei/) | Bimonthly from 1979, now built on JRA-3Q | Second ENSO view |
| PDO | [NCEI ersst.v5.pdo.dat](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/cmb/ersst/v5/index/ersst.v5.pdo.dat) | Monthly from 1854; missing values are 99.99 | Background band |
| AMO | [NCEI ersst.v5.amo.dat](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/cmb/ersst/v5/index/ersst.v5.amo.dat) | Monthly from 1854; no detrending stated | Context only |
| NAO, PNA, AO | CPC monthly files ([NAO](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/precip/CWlink/pna/norm.nao.monthly.b5001.current.ascii), [PNA](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/precip/CWlink/pna/norm.pna.monthly.b5001.current.ascii), [AO](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/precip/CWlink/daily_ao_index/monthly.ao.index.b50.current.ascii)) | Monthly from 1950 | Winter strips |

**Avoid two stale series that still look current.** The PSL Kaplan AMO stopped in January 2023 ([PSL AMO](https://psl.noaa.gov/data/timeseries/AMO/)). PSL's index list still points its PDO entry to a JISAO series labelled "using data from 1948 to 2002" ([PSL list](https://psl.noaa.gov/data/climateindices/list/)). Use the NCEI versions of both instead.

**Use RONI for ENSO, and say so.** NOAA switched because the relative index subtracts the average sea temperature of the global tropics. That makes it "less sensitive to the chosen base climatology period" and "more related to changes in rainfall over the tropical Pacific", which is what drives US impacts ([NWS PNS 26-05](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/pns26-05_Relative_ONI.pdf)). The two indices disagree on real winters in the app's window. RONI makes 2020–2023 one continuous La Niña, and it labels 2024–25 and 2025–26 La Niñas where the ONI shows neutral ([Drought.gov](https://www.drought.gov/news/new-noaa-el-nino-southern-oscillation-index-supports-drought-early-warning-2026-03-11)).

The researchers applied NOAA's five-overlapping-season rule to the RONI file themselves. It gives **7 El Niño winters (2003, 2005, 2007, 2010, 2016, 2019, 2024), 12 La Niña winters, 5 neutral winters and one borderline (2015)**. This is not an official NOAA table, and the borderline seasons should be checked against CPC's coloured version. Climate.gov is archived as of June 2025, but its ENSO pages remain the clearest US impact summary. Most US effects fall between October and March. The Gulf Coast is wetter in more than 80% of El Niño events, California's response depends on event strength, and the Ohio Valley typically runs drier ([Climate.gov, archived](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/blogs/enso/united-states-el-niño-impacts-0)).

**The slow ocean indices cannot be tested in 25 years.** The PDO was negative almost throughout 2001–2025, apart from 2014 to mid-2016, and it reached −4.21 in July 2025 ([NCEI PDO file](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/cmb/ersst/v5/index/ersst.v5.pdo.dat)). The NCEI AMO climbs from about 0.1–0.5 in 2001 to 0.7–1.0 in 2025, so over this window it behaves like a warming trend ([NCEI AMO file](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/cmb/ersst/v5/index/ersst.v5.amo.dat)). Correlating it with any local record that also trends, such as heat or declining NO₂, gives a large correlation that means nothing. Their real influence is multidecadal: McCabe and colleagues attribute **52% of the variance in multidecadal US drought frequency** to the PDO and AMO together ([PNAS 2004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC384707)). Show both as background bands, not tested drivers. NAO, PNA and AO change month to month and matter mostly in winter. The 2009–10 eastern winter, with the AO at −4.27 in February 2010, is the classic example inside the window ([CPC AO file](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/precip/CWlink/daily_ao_index/monthly.ao.index.b50.current.ascii)). The PNA is "strongly influenced by ENSO" ([CPC PNA](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/data/teledoc/pna.shtml)). Showing it next to ENSO as a separate "driver" counts one signal twice.

**The method should follow NOAA's own public conventions.** CPC's composites pair each mean map with a "percentage of years with the same sign" panel and report no significance test ([CPC readme](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/precip/CWlink/ENSO/composites/readme.shtml)). The researchers calculated that with 7 and 12 winters, a local difference must reach about **1.33 standard deviations** of that place's winter variability before it has an 80% chance of detection. "No clear link" will therefore be the honest default result for most places. La Niña winters also cluster late in the window (2021–2023, 2025), so a warming or drying trend can masquerade as a La Niña effect. Detrend each local record first.

Fire and smoke need a lag. After an autumn La Niña, the risk of extensive burns more than doubles the following spring in the Southern, Southwest and Rocky Mountain regions ([PreventionWeb, on Hoell et al.](https://www.preventionweb.net/news/phase-el-nino-southern-oscillation-foreshadows-us-wildfire-activity-year-advance)). So fire-season smoke in year Y should be compared with RONI for the previous September–November, a lag fixed in advance rather than found by scanning.

For any correlation, use an effective sample size: N_eff = N(1 − r₁r₂)/(1 + r₁r₂), where r₁ and r₂ are the lag-1 autocorrelations of the two series ([Bretherton et al. 1999](https://atmos.uw.edu/wallace/PDFs/1999%20-%20The%20effective%20number%20of%20spatial%20degrees%20of%20freedom%20of%20a%20time-varying%20field.pdf)). Apply the Benjamini–Hochberg correction across every index, season and lag tried, because testing each grid point separately overstates results ([Wilks 2016](https://bibsonomy.org/bibtex/282b206c5e7233eadc201d54af6279d72/pbett)). Draw ENSO as a red and blue strip under the monthly line, as CPC's tables do ([CPC RONI](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso/roni/)), never as a second y-axis. No published US study testing ENSO against PM2.5, smoke days or NO₂ was found, so any such result in Climatch is new, weak, place-level evidence and should be labelled that way.

## Eight pipeline steps deliver the maps within a 0.5 GB budget

The math in `frames()` already works on any grid. It applies shared class edges, change from the first five years, three-year trailing means for fires and smoke, and cos(latitude)-weighted means, and it ran in **1.5 s for PM2.5 on the full box** ([map_frames.py](../data_pipeline/tools/map_frames.py)). The work is in what feeds it and what it averages.

**The steps, in order:**

1. Replace the FIRMS-derived `us_mask` with the Census outline. Use `sources.power_region.inside`, sampling 5 × 5 points per cell ([power_region.py](../data_pipeline/sources/power_region.py)). This must come first.
2. Add `NA_BOX = {"lat": (10.0, 72.0), "lon": (-170.0, -50.0)}` beside `BOX`.
3. Rebuild the sources with wider constants **into new year folders and output names**. Every OPeNDAP builder skips a year whose file already exists, and `combine_years()` concatenates whatever is there, so reusing the old folders would mix two grids.
4. Bin finer grids onto the display grid by cell centre: `i = floor((lat − 9.75)/0.5)`, `j = floor((lon + 170.3125)/0.625)`, then a NaN-skipping mean per cell. This extends the NO₂ 2 × 2 block mean already in `map_frames.py`, with no new dependency.
5. Call `frames()` with separate masks: a `show_mask` that is painted, and a `mean_mask` on US cells that drives the "US average" line. Add `region_means` for named regions.
6. Take colour class edges from US (or land) cells only. Measured on PM2.5, the edges from the whole box are 3.1–12 µg/m³ and from US cells 3.7–11. Ocean cells would otherwise shift the familiar US colours and squeeze the change classes.
7. Write `demo_reports/map_frames_na/<field>.json` and add the keys to the backend `Literal` in [maps.py](../backend/api/v1/maps.py). The existing US files and API stay as they are.
8. Run `trend_field` on the box. It has about 8.6 times the cells, so the run takes minutes rather than the roughly one minute it takes today (this time is scaled, not measured). Keep a US-only false-discovery family so the US story doesn't shift.

These figures are estimates scaled from the repo's recorded builds ([DATA_ADDITIONS.md](../docs/DATA_ADDITIONS.md)). The FIRMS figures are from HTTP HEAD requests, and download times from the venue were never timed. The access pattern should stay the one the repo already trusts: `earthaccess` login, one DAP4 constraint per granule, and a grid check before saving ([merra2_aod.py](../data_pipeline/sources/merra2_aod.py)). New builders should call `earthaccess.login()` without `persist=True`, which would leave a password in `~/.netrc`. NASA's Harmony subsetter would add asynchronous job polling and buy nothing here ([Harmony docs](https://harmony.earthdata.nasa.gov/docs)).

**The browser side needs four small changes.**

- **Smaller cells on screen.** At the current 8 px per cell, a box frame is a 2.0-megapixel canvas, and 25 decoded frames take about 200 MB. Setting `S = 4` when the grid has more than 120 columns brings that back to about 50 MB, close to today's footprint ([fieldPaint.js](../frontend/src/lib/fieldPaint.js)).
- **Row height from the box.** The fixed 1.3 Mercator ratio should be computed from the box's top and bottom latitudes. For 10–72°N it is about 1.54, and rows at 72°N draw about 3.2 times taller than rows at 10°N.
- **Masks and outlines.** Fields defined over land and sea (AOD, smoke, NO₂, temperature) should colour the whole box, with the US outline stroked on top. The existing `addLandMask` works for fields that are valid only in downloaded countries ([basemap.js](../frontend/src/lib/basemap.js)). Pass it the US (Census) rings plus Canada and Mexico rings from Natural Earth, which is public domain and draws de facto borders ([Natural Earth terms](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/about/terms-of-use/)).
- **Visible "no data".** Show no-data cells as a light grey with a legend swatch, as Climate.gov's land–ocean maps do ([Climate.gov Data Snapshots](https://content-drupal.climate.gov/maps-data/data-snapshots/data-source/temperature-global-monthly-difference-average)), not the same white as "outside the map".

**Some fields need surface-specific handling.** PM2.5 over open sea is mostly sea salt, which the formula includes ([pm25_merra2.py](../data_pipeline/sources/pm25_merra2.py)), so mask it to land. Keep AOD and smoke over the sea, where continuity shows Canadian smoke heading out over the Atlantic.

**Each field should carry a coverage line in its JSON.** For example: "Fire detections: US, Canada and Mexico only." Count fires per 1° square: those squares shrink to a third of their equatorial area by 70°N, so label the squares or divide by area.

**The time series beside the map are cheap.** Each is computed with the same cos(latitude) weights used for state means. Canadian fire detections per year are a row count of one parquet file. Gulf of Mexico sea temperature is an area-weighted OISST mean inside a hand-drawn polygon, computed at native 0.25° with OISST's land mask so coastal cells don't bias it. Keep the "US average" line, add named region lines, and **never average land and ocean into one "North America" number**.

## Conclusion

The North America extension is a cropping and masking job, not a data hunt. The binding constraints are a mask bug and the honesty of 2001–2025 trends across known product breaks; data availability and the browser budget do not bind. The best storytelling value per megabyte comes from four additions: OISST (Gulf and Pacific heat), the Canada FIRMS file (the 2023 smoke year), HURDAT2 tracks with ACE, and a RONI strip. Together they cost a few hundred megabytes, mostly the fire files, need no new account, and turn an outline of the lower 48 into a picture of where US air and weather come from. The products to avoid are the expensive or account-bound ones that add little over MERRA-2 and OISST at a 50 km display grid: TROPOMI L2, Daymet daily, MCD64A1 tiles, MODIS LST, and the ECMWF-gated ERA5-Land and CAMS.

Three decisions remain for the team, not the data. First, how far south the box goes: 10°N includes the Central American smoke sources, and 14°N drops them. Second, whether ENSO results are stated on RONI with an ONI check, or the other way round. Third, whether the trend map's false-discovery family stays US-only. Several unknowns should be checked before relying on the numbers above. Nobody has verified how evenly HMS analysts cover northern Canada. The OMI download time is an untested docstring estimate. The trend-map runtime on 24,125 cells and `JSON.parse` time for 5–6 MB on a phone were not measured. Measure those before the demo, not after.
