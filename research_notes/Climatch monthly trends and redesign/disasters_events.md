# Detecting, labelling and explaining disasters and extreme events in US place time series (Climatch)

Scope: one US place (lat/lon) at a time; NASA POWER daily weather 2001–2025 (T, RH, precipitation), MERRA-2 PM2.5 monthly, OMI NO2 monthly, FIRMS MODIS fire detections 2003–2025, MODIS LST. Status checked 14 Nov 2026. No forecasts and no medical advice.

## Q1. Event-detection methods that work on one place's daily, weekly and monthly series (and which are easy in Python from NASA POWER)

### Takeaway
Every core method here can be computed in a few lines of pandas from POWER daily T2M_MAX/T2M_MIN/T2M, RH2M and PRECTOTCORR. Use calendar-day percentile thresholds with a moving window (ETCCDI TX90p style), add run-length rules (3 or more days for heatwaves, 6 or more for WSDI), then calendar-month z-scores and SPI for monthly views. The Excess Heat Factor needs only daily mean temperature. Smoke flags work best as "PM2.5/AOD anomaly vs a season-specific median" plus a satellite plume check (HMS), because anomaly-only flags misattribute other pollution.

### Cited Findings
**Percentile extremes and ETCCDI indices**
- ETCCDI publishes the official list of 27 core indices. CDD is the largest number of consecutive days with precipitation < 1 mm, and CWD is the largest number of consecutive days with precipitation ≥ 1 mm. — [ETCCDI list of 27 indices](https://etccdi.pacificclimate.org/list_27_indices.shtml)
- TX90p is the percentage of days whose daily maximum temperature exceeds the 90th percentile. The percentile is computed for each calendar day from a base period using a 5-day moving window. — [Copernicus CDS, extreme indices CMIP6](https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/sis-extreme-indices-cmip6)
- WSDI (warm spell duration index) counts the days in a year that are part of runs of at least 6 consecutive days with TX above the 90th percentile. — [arXiv 2503.22807 (index table)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.22807); [ETCCDI list](https://etccdi.pacificclimate.org/list_27_indices.shtml)
- R95p is the annual total precipitation from days above the 95th percentile of wet-day precipitation. — [CDO ECA reference manual](https://sources.debian.org/src/cdo/2.6.0-1/doc/cdo_eca.pdf)
- The standard reference code for the 27 indices is the climdex.pcic(.ncdf) library, which is an R library. ESMValTool wraps it. — [ESMValTool extreme-events recipe](https://esmvaltool.readthedocs.io/en/latest/recipes/recipe_extreme_events.html)

**Heatwave definitions**
- NWS has no single national heatwave definition. A heat wave is "a period of abnormally hot weather generally lasting more than two days", and thresholds depend on local climate (four 100 °F days in Phoenix would not count). — [Midwestern Regional Climate Center, heatwaves](https://mrcc.purdue.edu/living_wx/heatwaves); [La Grande Observer, NWS explainer](https://lagrandeobserver.com/?p=199169)
- The general Excessive Heat Warning rule is a maximum heat index of 105 °F or higher for at least 2 days with nighttime lows of 75 °F or more, but criteria vary by NWS office. New York State uses a heat index of 105 °F or more for 2 or more hours, and Chicago uses impact-based criteria developed after 1995. — [NWS Albany NY heat fact sheet](https://www.weather.gov/media/aly/FactSheets/NYSHeat.pdf); [NWS Chicago heat preparedness](https://www.weather.gov/lot/heatprepare)
- NWS HeatRisk is a color-numeric 0–4 index that complements, rather than replaces, the traditional heat products. NWS may issue a Heat Advisory or an Excessive Heat Warning based on HeatRisk. Newer NWS pages use "Extreme Heat Warning" wording. — [WPC HeatRisk, Understanding HeatRisk](https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/heatrisk/understanding.html); [NWS Chicago](https://www.weather.gov/lot/heatprepare)
- Perkins and Alexander (2013, J. Climate) define a heatwave as 3 or more consecutive days above one of three thresholds: the 90th percentile of TX, the 90th percentile of TN, or positive EHF. In the related 2012 paper TX90pct marks daytime heatwaves and TN90pct marks nighttime ones. — [ANU repository, "On the measurement of heat waves"](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/on-the-measurement-of-heat-waves/); [UNSW repository](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/2a9eb4c2-0a6a-4015-b2f2-11f21f88c9ca/full)
- Excess Heat Factor (Nairn and Fawcett 2015) has two parts. EHIsig is the 3-day mean of daily mean temperature minus the 95th-percentile climatology. EHIaccl is the 3-day mean minus the previous 30-day mean. EHF = EHIsig × max(1, EHIaccl), and heatwave conditions exist when EHF > 0. The Australian Bureau of Meteorology uses this method. — [Nairn & Fawcett 2015, IJERPH (PMC4306859)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4306859); [Queensland Long Paddock factsheet 8](https://longpaddock.qld.gov.au/qld-future-climate/factsheets/8)

**Drought indices**
- The `climate_indices` Python package (PyPI, current wheel 2.0.1) is described as a community reference implementation of SPI and SPEI with gamma and Pearson Type III fitting. It also provides PET by Thornthwaite or Hargreaves, PNP, and Palmer indices (PDSI, scPDSI, PHDI, Z-Index). It began as a fork of code maintained by NIDIS/NCEI/NOAA. — [PyPI climate-indices](https://pypi.org/project/climate-indices); [GitHub fork README](https://github.com/GvdDool/climate_indices)

**NASA POWER inputs**
- Most POWER meteorological parameters come from MERRA-2 on its native grid of 1/2° latitude × 5/8° longitude. PRECTOTCORR is "MERRA-2 Precipitation Corrected (mm/day)". POWER also offers a 0.1° IMERG precipitation alternative, daily only and in UTC, with the Final Run about 3.5 months behind. — [POWER data sources](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/methodology/data/sources/); [POWER FAQ](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/faqs/data/); [nasapower R client README](https://packages.oit.ncsu.edu/cran/web/packages/nasapower/readme/README.html)
- POWER advises ending climate-trend analyses about 2 months before real time, because the latest period comes from the GEOS-IT extension. — [POWER data sources](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/methodology/data/sources/)

**Smoke flags from PM2.5/AOD anomalies**
- Burke et al. (2023) compute daily PM2.5 anomalies against season- and period-specific median concentrations at each location. They attribute an anomaly to wildfire only when satellite imagery (NOAA HMS) shows a plume overhead that day. Non-smoke PM2.5 is the remainder. — [Burke, Childs et al. 2023 (PDF)](https://web.stanford.edu/~mburke/papers/BurkeChilds_etal_2023.pdf)
- Childs et al. (2022, ES&T 56(19)) is a machine-learning model that combines ground, satellite and reanalysis data. It gives daily smoke PM2.5 on a 10 km grid for the contiguous US, 2006–2020. — [Childs et al. 2022 (PDF)](https://web.stanford.edu/~mburke/papers/ChildsEtAl2022_smoke.pdf)
- A critique: using the non-smoke-day median as the background is "imperfect", and elevated PM2.5 from other sources can be wrongly attributed to smoke. Counting HMS light-density plumes as smoke days greatly overestimates smoke days and their trends. — [Wildland Fire (CSIRO) WF23148](https://www.publish.csiro.au/WF/fulltext/WF23148)

### Inferences
- **Easy in pandas from POWER daily (no extra libraries):**
  1. *Calendar-day percentile flags (TX90p/TN90p style).* For each day-of-year, pool the values within ±2 days (5-day window, ETCCDI) or ±7 days (15-day window, common in heatwave papers; unconfirmed here) across all base years. Take the 90th percentile and flag days above it. Using 2001–2025 as both base period and analysis period is "in-sample". ETCCDI normally uses a fixed base (e.g., 1961–1990) with bootstrapping, so state plainly "relative to this place's 2001–2025 climate".
  2. *Run-length rule.* Group consecutive flagged days with `(flag != flag.shift()).cumsum()`. Keep runs of 3 or more days (Perkins–Alexander heatwave) or 6 or more days (WSDI). Report start date, length, peak value and mean exceedance as an "event".
  3. *EHF.* Compute T = (T2M_MAX + T2M_MIN)/2 (or POWER T2M daily mean), the 3-day rolling mean, the prior 30-day mean and the 95th percentile over all days. This is about 6 lines. EHF > 0 for 3 or more days gives a heatwave, and the EHF sum gives intensity.
  4. *CDD/CWD/R95p/Rx1day.* Run lengths of PRECTOTCORR < 1 or ≥ 1 mm/day, and wet-day 95th-percentile sums. Each is a few lines.
  5. *Calendar-month standardized anomaly (z-score).* `z = (x − mean_month)/std_month`, computed per calendar month across years so seasonality is removed. Flag |z| ≥ 2 as "unusual for this month". For skewed variables (precipitation, PM2.5, FIRMS counts) use percentile rank, log-transform, or robust z = (x − median)/(1.4826·MAD). This is the best fit for the existing month-by-year heatmap: colour cells by z or percentile instead of raw values.
  6. *SPI-1/3/6/12* from PRECTOTCORR monthly totals via `climate_indices` (or scipy gamma fit per calendar month). SPEI needs PET. Hargreaves PET needs only T2M_MAX, T2M_MIN and extraterrestrial radiation (computable from latitude and day of year), so SPEI is feasible from POWER alone. 25 years is short for a stable gamma fit; label SPI as "relative to 2001–2025".
  7. *Heat index.* POWER has T2M and RH2M, so the NWS heat index (Rothfusz regression; formula not verified in this pass) can be computed daily. Flag days at HI ≥ 105 °F as "near the typical NWS warning level" and note that offices differ.
- **Smoke flag for monthly MERRA-2 PM2.5 / MODIS AOD:** z-score or percentile vs the same calendar month (e.g., June 2023 vs all Junes). Optionally confirm with an HMS plume over the point on 1 or more days that month and with FIRMS counts that are not local (smoke can come from Canada while local FIRMS stays at zero; the brief's Wisconsin example). Use language such as "consistent with smoke", never "caused by".
- OMI NO2 is a combustion/traffic tracer. It is a weak smoke flag, but it shows non-disaster "events" such as the spring-2020 COVID drop (not verified in this pass).
- If daily FIRMS detections are counted within a radius of the place, a "fire event" can be a run of 1 or more days with counts above the calendar-month 95th percentile.

### Gaps
- The exact Perkins–Alexander percentile window (15-day) and the "CTX90pct" naming were not confirmed. Reports say CTX90pct comes from later work (e.g., Fischer & Schär 2010) but this was not verified.
- WMO/WHO heatwave guidance (2015 "Heatwaves and Health") was not fetched.
- McKee et al. (1993) SPI time-scale conventions were not confirmed from a primary source.
- The Python `xclim` library (ETCCDI-style indices on xarray) is a likely alternative to R climdex, but it was not verified in this pass.
- I found no study that validates MERRA-2 surface PM2.5 against US monitors specifically during smoke episodes (see Q5).

## Q2. Free event catalogs to cross-check and label spikes: status in 2026, licence/attribution, API keys

### Takeaway
All needed catalogs are free and most need no key. EONET, OpenFEMA, the USDM REST API and NIFC Open Data need no login. FIRMS needs a free MAP_KEY. HMS smoke and Storm Events are bulk files. The big change: NOAA retired the Billion-Dollar Disasters product in May 2025 (archive 1980–2024 stays online), and Climate Central relaunched it on 22 Oct 2025 with the same method and the same lead scientist. EONET is not a long historical catalog: US events appear from about 2013 onward, so it cannot label 2001–2012 spikes.

### Cited Findings
**NASA EONET v3**
- Base URL `https://eonet.gsfc.nasa.gov/api/v3`. Endpoints: `/events`, `/events/geojson`, `/events/rss`, `/events/atom`, `/categories`, `/sources`, `/layers`, `/magnitudes`. Event parameters: `source`, `category`, `status` (open|closed|all; default open), `limit`, `days`, `start`, `end` (YYYY-MM-DD, inclusive), `magID`, `magMin`, `magMax`, `bbox` (upper-left corner first). The docs page mentions no API key. Example: `https://eonet.gsfc.nasa.gov/api/v3/events?limit=5&days=20&source=InciWeb&status=open`. — [EONET v3 docs](https://eonet.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/v3)
- The 13 categories (ids): drought, dustHaze, earthquakes, floods, landslides, manmade, seaLakeIce, severeStorms, snow, tempExtremes, volcanoes, waterColor, wildfires. — [EONET categories endpoint](https://eonet.gsfc.nasa.gov/api/v3/categories)
- Test query (14 Nov 2026), US bbox `-125,50,-66,24`, `status=all`, `start=2001-01-01`, `end=2018-12-31`: the newest results include "Camp Fire, California", first geometry 2018-11-08T06:33Z, with sources CALFIRE, EO, InciWeb and PDC. — [EONET query](https://eonet.gsfc.nasa.gov/api/v3/events?status=all&start=2001-01-01&end=2018-12-31&bbox=-125,50,-66,24&limit=30)
- A query ending 2014-12-31 returned mostly hurricanes and tropical storms from 2013–2014 (e.g., EONET_1180 Tropical Storm Andrea, 2013-06-05) and one wildfire (Happy Camp Complex, 2014). The earliest visible event was June 2013, but the response was truncated. — [EONET query](https://eonet.gsfc.nasa.gov/api/v3/events?status=all&start=2000-01-01&end=2014-12-31&bbox=-125,50,-66,24&limit=50)

**NASA FIRMS**
- Area API requests need a free MAP_KEY, issued by email from the FIRMS sign-up page. The limit is 5,000 transactions per 10 minutes, and larger requests (e.g., 7 days) count as several transactions. — [NASA Applied Sciences FIRMS 2025 Q&A](https://appliedsciences.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-05/Part%203%20FIRMS%202025%20Q&A%20Session.docx.pdf); [FIRMS API map_key page](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/api/map_key)
- For data older than 7 days, submit an Archive Download request. — [ARSET FIRMS homework (2026)](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/s3fs-public/2026-06/arset-firmsintro2025-homework.pdf)
- The `/api/data_availability/` endpoint reports date coverage per product. — [FIRMS API area/csv](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/api/area/csv)

**NOAA HMS smoke polygons**
- Daily shapefiles are at `.../Smoke_Polygons/Shapefile/YYYY/MM/hms_smokeYYYYMMDD.zip`, with an older archive at `satepsanone.nesdis.noaa.gov/pub/volcano/FIRE/HMS_ARCHIVE/`. Coverage starts mid-2005. — [WildfireData.jl HMS module](https://rallypointone.github.io/WildfireData.jl/dev/modules/hms.html); [tianjialiu/HMS-Smoke](https://github.imc.re/tianjialiu/HMS-Smoke)
- Analysts began assigning density classes (light/medium/heavy, by perceived opacity) in June 2010. Polygons from 2005–2007, 2009 and parts of 2008 and 2010 have no density class. — [EPA 2026 document](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-02/2026-02.pdf); [HMS-Smoke project](https://github.imc.re/tianjialiu/HMS-Smoke)

**NOAA NCEI Storm Events Database**
- Bulk gzipped CSVs per year (details, locations, fatalities) are at `https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/swdi/stormevents/csvfiles/`, named like `StormEvents_details-ftp_v1.0_dYYYY_cYYYYMMDD.csv.gz`. Recent files carry 2026 creation dates (e.g., c20260323). — [NCEI csvfiles directory](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/swdi/stormevents/csvfiles/); [Storm Events FTP page](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/stormevents/ftp.jsp)
- Data run from January 1950 (the page last seen said "to October 2025"). Since 1996, 48 event types are recorded under NWS Directive 10-1605; 1950–1954 has tornadoes only, and 1955–1995 has tornado, thunderstorm wind and hail. — [Storm Events details](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/stormevents/details.jsp); [Storm Events FTP page](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/stormevents/ftp.jsp)
- A Source Coop mirror reports 55 event types. This conflicts with NCEI's "48". — [Source Coop storm-events-db](https://source.coop/earthgenome/storm-events-db)

**NOAA NCEI Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters**
- Notice dated 8 May 2025: the product "will be retired, with no updates beyond calendar year 2024". Past reports (1980–2024) and their data "remain authoritative, archived, and available". NOAA cited "evolving priorities, statutory mandates, and staffing changes". — [Fox Weather](https://www.foxweather.com/weather-news/billion-dollar-database-retirement-2025-noaa); [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/u-s-stops-tracking-costs-of-extreme-weather-disasters-fueled-by-climate-change)

First-half 2025: 14 separate $1B+ disasters totalling $101.4B, with the January LA wildfires the costliest at $61.2B (the costliest US wildfires on record). — [KTLA/Cision press release](https://digital-release.ktla.com/business/press-releases/cision/20251022DC04519/billion-dollar-disaster-dataset-returns-now-at-climate-central); [Good Morning America](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/billion-dollar-disaster-data-returns-time-run-federal-126763532); [Fox Weather](https://www.foxweather.com/weather-news/noaa-billion-dollar-disaster-database-returns-climate-central)

**US Drought Monitor (USDM)**
- REST base `https://usdmdataservices.unl.edu/api/`. County pattern: `CountyStatistics/{GetDroughtSeverityStatisticsByArea | ...ByAreaPercent | ...ByPopulation | ...ByPopulationPercent | GetDSCI}`. Parameters: `aoi` (5-digit county FIPS, or a state code for all its counties), `startdate`/`enddate` in M/D/YYYY, and `statisticsType` (1 traditional, 2 categorical). - Drought.gov hosts county drought data on Google Cloud Storage, free to download. — [Drought.gov location data](https://www.drought.gov/location/data)

**OpenFEMA Disaster Declarations Summaries v2**
- Endpoint `https://www.fema.gov/api/open/v2/DisasterDeclarationsSummaries` (CSV, JSON, JSONA, JSONL, Parquet). Fields include declarationDate, incidentType, incidentBeginDate, incidentEndDate, state, designatedArea, fipsStateCode, and fipsCountyCode (`000` = statewide). Declarations start in 1953, county-level from 1964. The data refresh about every 20 minutes and were last refreshed 11-14-2026. Citation rules are on the OpenFEMA Terms and Conditions page. **NIFC wildfire perimeters**
- NIFC Open Data (`data-nifc.opendata.arcgis.com`) needs no login. InterAgencyFirePerimeterHistory (All Years) combines agency perimeters "thru the 2024 fire season", and minimum-size rules vary by agency. The WFIGS Interagency Perimeters layer holds only certified perimeters and perimeters captured from 2021 onward. A separate "WFIGS – Wildland Fire Perimeters Full History" layer exists and "may be incomplete". The HIFLD Open mirror was deactivated on 26 Aug 2025. — [NWCG PMS 936 public distribution](https://nwcg.gov/publications/pms936/national-incident-feature-services/public-distribution); [AmeriGEOSS catalog: InterAgencyFirePerimeterHistory](https://data.amerigeoss.org/dataset/interagencyfireperimeterhistory-all-years-view); [WIFIRE catalog: WFIGS Full History](https://wifire-data.sdsc.edu/dataset/wfigs-wildland-fire-perimeters-full-history); [PSU metadata](https://www.datacommons.psu.edu/metadata/WFIGS%20Interagency%20Fire%20Perimeters-metadata.html); [Data Rescue Project](https://portal.datarescueproject.org/datasets/hifld-open-wfigs-interagency-fire-perimeters/)

### Inferences
- **Best labelling stack for 2001–2025 at one point:** (1) Storm Events for county or NWS-zone heat, flood, drought, wildfire and winter events back to 2001; (2) OpenFEMA for declared disasters by county, with incident begin and end dates; (3) USDM weekly county drought percentages, a natural "drought shading" layer since 2000; (4) FIRMS and NIFC perimeters for fires; (5) HMS for smoke days from mid-2005; (6) EONET only as a recent-years (about 2013+) cross-check with nice titles and links; (7) the billion-dollar list (NCEI archive to 2024, Climate Central from 2025) for "headline" events with cost.
- Cross-check rule: a spike is "labelled" when a catalog event of a compatible type overlaps its time window (e.g., ±3 days for daily data, the same month for monthly) and space (county FIPS of the point, a perimeter within X km, or the point inside an HMS polygon). Otherwise call it "unusual, no matching catalog event". This avoids implying causes the app cannot show.
- Storm Events event types likely include "Excessive Heat", "Heat", "Drought", "Wildfire", "Flash Flood", "Winter Storm", "Extreme Cold/Wind Chill" and "Dense Smoke" (from general knowledge of Directive 10-1605; check the bulk-format PDF).
- For a local demo, pre-download the bulk files (Storm Events CSVs, HMS shapefiles, billion-dollar CSV) and cache API pulls (OpenFEMA, USDM). No keys are needed except the FIRMS MAP_KEY, which the app already has or does not need if FIRMS is cached.

### Gaps
- Licence wording was not captured for FIRMS (NASA LANCE), EONET, USDM ("Permission" page not read), OpenFEMA (Terms page not read) or HMS. NASA and NOAA data are generally public domain or open with attribution requested, but this was not verified per source.
- Whether OpenFEMA needs an API key is not stated on the dataset page. It is widely known to be keyless; not verified.
- The earliest EONET event date is unconfirmed (responses were truncated). The tests suggest no US events before about 2013.
- The latest Storm Events coverage end date in Nov 2026 is not confirmed (the page I saw said Oct 2025).
- Climate Central's update status in 2026 (any full-2025 list) was not confirmed. NCEI's own wording on the archive landing page was seen only via news quotes.
- Whether the NIFC perimeter history has been extended past the 2024 season was not confirmed.

## Q3. Well-documented US disasters 2001–2025: dates, places, magnitudes and the best-signal variable in Climatch

### Takeaway
Large-scale, multi-day events (Canadian smoke 2023, western smoke 2020, PNW heat dome 2021, Texas freeze 2021, the 2011/2012/2022 droughts) show clearly in Climatch's daily or monthly data. Point-scale events (Camp Fire, LA fires, the Harvey and Helene rainfall maxima) are real but small or extreme relative to a ~50 km cell. The app should show them through FIRMS counts, 1 km LST and daily precipitation, and say the gridded peak will be lower than the station record.

## Q4. Presenting events on time series, and separating "a disaster spike" from "a trend" honestly

### Takeaway
Show events as layers on top of the series, not as part of the trend: point markers or shaded date spans, with an "event card" that cites a catalog. Then report trends robustly. Sen's slope plus Mann–Kendall is already resistant to one outlier year. Also show the trend with and without flagged event years, and say "this trend depends on 2023" whenever removing one year flips significance. Some real trends are made of events (e.g., western smoke PM2.5 rising), so "events" and "trend" are not always opposites.

### Cited Findings
- The Theil–Sen estimator tolerates arbitrary corruption of up to about 29.3% of points in simple 2-D regression. The repeated-median estimator reaches 50%. — [scikit-learn Theil–Sen example](https://scikit-learn.org/1.5/auto_examples/linear_model/plot_theilsen.html); [Theil–Sen estimator overview](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Slope_selection)
- Caveat: Theil–Sen's breakdown can technically be zero when a bad point has high leverage (an extreme predictor value). — [pyrobustlm breakdown-point notebook](https://pyrobustlm.readthedocs.io/en/stable/notebooks/03_breakdown.html)
- Wildfire smoke is reversing decades of US air-quality gains, and smoke now drives PM2.5 trends in much of the West. So event-driven increases can themselves be the trend. — [Stanford News, Sept 2022](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2022/09/wildfire-smoke-unraveling-decades-air-quality-gains); [Burke, Childs et al. 2023](https://web.stanford.edu/~mburke/papers/BurkeChilds_etal_2023.pdf)
- Light HMS plumes inflate smoke-day counts and their trends, so trend claims built on smoke flags depend on the flag definition. — [WF23148](https://www.publish.csiro.au/WF/fulltext/WF23148)
- Philip et al. stress that the June 2021 temperatures lay far outside the historical range, so statistical return-period estimates for record-shattering events are very uncertain. — [Philip et al. 2022](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/13/1689/2022/)

### Inferences
- **Honest trend checks to compute (all cheap):**
  1. Sen's slope plus Mann–Kendall on annual values (current method).
  2. **Leave-one-out sensitivity:** recompute the slope and p-value 25 times, each without one year. Report the range, and flag "fragile" if any single removal changes the sign or crosses p = 0.05.
  3. **With and without event years:** drop the years with catalog-confirmed events (or |z| ≥ 3) and show both slopes side by side.
  4. **Endpoint effect:** an extreme year at the start or end of a 25-year record has the most leverage on OLS and still moves Sen's slope more than a mid-record extreme. Warn when the max or min year is the first or last 3 years (e.g., a 2023 smoke spike near the end of 2001–2025, or 2025 LA fires as the final year).
  5. **Trend in frequency vs trend in mean:** for event-driven variables (PM2.5, FIRMS), also test the count of exceedance days or months per year (Poisson or MK on counts). This answers "are spikes becoming more frequent?" separately from "is the typical level changing?".
  6. Use the median of annual values or the annual 50th percentile alongside the annual mean; one extreme month moves the mean a lot and the median little.
- **Visual patterns (suggested, not sourced):**
  - Daily/weekly line: thin grey line plus a shaded climatological band (10th–90th percentile for that calendar day) so exceedances "pop". Detected runs become shaded vertical spans coloured by type (heat = red, cold = blue, wet = teal, dry = brown, smoke = grey).
  - Monthly heatmap (already in the app): colour by calendar-month z or percentile, outline cells with |z| ≥ 2, and put a small icon in cells that match a catalog event (EONET/Storm Events/FEMA/HMS).
  - Annual trend chart: hollow markers for event years plus two lines (all years vs without event years), with a note such as "Trend with 2023: +x/decade (p = …); without: +y/decade (p = …)".
  - **Event card** (on click): name; dates; what the data show here (value, percentile, z, "highest June in 25 years"); what catalogs say (link, type, county, source); a caveat line on resolution or "consistent with, not proof of". No health advice: link to official AQI/NWS pages for safety guidance.
- Wording that separates the two: "Spike = a short, unusual departure from what's normal for that time of year. Trend = a slow change in the typical level over many years. One big spike can make a trend look stronger than it is, so we show the trend with and without it."

### Gaps
- No primary sources were fetched on how NOAA Climate at a Glance, NASA Worldview or Our World in Data annotate events. The visual guidance above is design inference.
- No source was found that quantifies Mann–Kendall/Sen sensitivity to a single extreme year in 20–30-year records. The reasoning above is unsourced.

## Q5. Which disasters are visible in ~50 km gridded data (MERRA-2/POWER) vs 1 km data

### Takeaway
POWER/MERRA-2 meteorology sits on a 0.5° × 0.625° grid (about 55 × 50 km, ~2,800–3,000 km² at mid-latitudes). Synoptic-scale events (heat domes, cold outbreaks, multi-week droughts, long-range smoke) appear at full strength. Fire footprints and convective or orographic rain maxima are sub-grid and get smoothed. Use FIRMS (1 km detections) and MODIS LST (1 km) for local fires and burn scars, and POWER's 0.1° IMERG option for extreme rain.

### Cited Findings
- POWER keeps MERRA-2's native 1/2° latitude × 5/8° longitude for meteorological parameters, and offers IMERG precipitation at 0.1° (~10 km), daily, as an alternative. — [POWER data sources](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/methodology/data/sources/); [POWER FAQ](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/faqs/data/)
- MERRA-2 surface PM2.5 underestimates mass and exceedance days against ground monitors at 20 Indian stations (2015–2018), partly because of limits in the reanalysis method. — [Springer AJAE 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.5572/ajae.2022.039.pdf); [ACP 21:14427 (2021)](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/21/14427/2021/)
- MERRA (v1) aerosol reanalysis evaluated against EPA/IMPROVE US monitors showed species-dependent PM2.5 biases. Single-wavelength AOD assimilation cannot fix errors in vertical placement or composition, which are central for smoke. — [MERRAero US evaluation record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c444t-721179dce6fd87ed4ef6aa96e2634b53bb3ba0b4ac03c57ffba7183bca48cd543/Details)
- In the western US, chemical transport models overestimated PM2.5 by up to 3–5× during extreme smoke episodes, while ML estimates matched monitors better. EPA monitors are mostly urban. — [PubMed 39694472 (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39694472/); [PMC10288567](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10288567)
- Burn areas for scale: Camp Fire 153,336 acres ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/2018_Camp_Fire)); Palisades 23,448 acres and Eaton 14,021 acres ([LA County](https://file.lacounty.gov/SDSInter/lac/1199046_Eaton_Palisades_Fires_Anniversary_Fact_Sheet.pdf)). The PNW heat-dome study region spans 45–52°N, 119–123°W ([Philip et al. 2022](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/13/1689/2022/)).

### Inferences
- **Grid-cell arithmetic:** 0.5° lat ≈ 55.5 km. 0.625° lon ≈ 53 km at 40°N and ≈ 48 km at 45°N, so one POWER/MERRA-2 cell is about 2,700–3,000 km². Camp Fire ≈ 620 km², about 20% of a cell. Palisades ≈ 95 km² and Eaton ≈ 57 km², together about 5% of a cell. Burn scars and fire heat are therefore sub-grid in POWER T2M. FIRMS points and 1 km MODIS LST will show them.
- **Visibility matrix (expected):**
  - Clearly visible at ~50 km, daily and monthly: PNW heat dome 2021 (region ~780 × 300 km); Texas freeze 2021 (state-wide); Canadian smoke 2023 and western smoke 2020 (plumes spanning hundreds to thousands of km; monthly PM2.5/AOD); Texas 2011, Plains 2012 and western 2020–22 droughts (SPI, multi-month).
  - Visible but damped at ~50 km: Harvey and Helene rain (true multi-day totals, but cell means are much lower than 60.58 in / 30.78 in point maxima; orographic WNC rain is smoothed most); Katrina rain (surge, the main killer, is not in any Climatch variable).
  - Mostly invisible at ~50 km, visible at 1 km: individual wildfires (Camp, Palisades, Eaton) as local heat and burn scars. Use FIRMS counts within a radius and MODIS LST before/after anomalies. Their smoke can still show in monthly PM2.5 downwind.
- **Temporal aggregation also hides events:** a 3-day event is at most 10% of a monthly mean. For example, one 117 µg/m³ smoke day in NYC moves the June mean by only a few µg/m³ unless other days are also smoky. So daily POWER detection (runs) should feed monthly views as "event-day counts per month", not only as monthly means.
- MERRA-2 PM2.5 is model-plus-assimilation. Expect it to under-represent peak smoke at the surface and to smear plume edges. Label smoke spikes as "relative" (vs the same month in other years), not as absolute exposure, and never imply health advice.

### Gaps
- No direct evaluation of MERRA-2 surface PM2.5 against US monitors during the 2020 or 2023 smoke episodes was found.
- Whether POWER's IMERG option covers 2001–2025 fully (IMERG Final starts in 2000) and how it handles the 0.1° cell for a lat/lon request was not confirmed. The POWER docs and slides differ on IMERG start dates.
- The native resolution of the app's MODIS AOD and LST products (e.g., MOD08 1° monthly vs MCD19A2 1 km AOD; MOD11 1 km LST) was not checked. This matters for which product shows local fires.
